Every few months, the same argument reappears: the Fediverse cannot scale.

Sometimes this comes from people defending centralized platforms. Sometimes it comes from people excited about newer protocols like Bluesky’s AT Protocol. Sometimes it comes from frustrated users who bounced off Mastodon because choosing an instance felt confusing, moderation norms felt opaque, or the network seemed too fragmented.

There is some truth in the criticism. The Fediverse can be confusing. ActivityPub-based networks are not always seamless. Discovery can be uneven. Account migration is still imperfect. Some instances are run by exhausted volunteers who are doing essential public infrastructure work with the budget and staffing of a group chat.

But I think the conclusion people draw from this is often wrong.

The problem is not that the Fediverse cannot scale. The problem is that we have not built enough durable institutions around it.

If we want federated social media to become a realistic alternative for normal users, not just technically curious early adopters, then the next step is not another venture-backed platform promising future decentralization. It is not every user running their own server. It is not pretending unpaid moderation labor can hold up the public square forever.

The next step is boring, practical, and achievable:

Large, professionally run, regionally based nonprofit instances.

## Most People Do Not Want to Choose Infrastructure

One of the recurring weaknesses of Mastodon and the broader Fediverse is onboarding.

A new user shows up and immediately has to answer a question they have probably never considered before: “Which server do you want to join?”

For experienced users, this can be empowering. Your instance can reflect your interests, language, region, politics, moderation expectations, or community norms. That is one of the Fediverse’s strengths.

But for a casual user, it can be paralyzing.

Most people do not want to pick infrastructure before they know whether they even like the network. They do not want to read instance rules, compare federation policies, evaluate admin reputations, or understand domain-based identity. They want to make an account, follow people, post, read, and see if the place is worth their time.

This is not a moral failing. It is normal.

The Fediverse often assumes users should care about infrastructure because infrastructure matters. But the reality is that most people only care after something goes wrong. They care when a platform gets bought by a billionaire. They care when an algorithm changes overnight. They care when moderation collapses. They care when APIs are shut off, communities are destroyed, or years of social graph are held hostage.

By then, it is usually too late.

So the question is: how do we give casual users a simple default while preserving their ability to leave?

## The Case for Regional Nonprofit Instances

A realistic model would look something like this:

- Large nonprofit instances organized by region, country, language, or continent
- Paid administrators, moderators, and infrastructure staff
- Transparent governance and published policies
- Community funding through memberships, donations, grants, and sponsorships
- No surveillance advertising as the core business model
- Easy migration paths for users who later want to move to smaller or more specialized communities

Think of these instances as public-interest onramps.

A new user could join something like a large regional general-purpose instance without needing to understand the entire Fediverse on day one. They could stay there forever if they wanted. That should be considered a valid outcome. Not everyone needs to become a power user. Not everyone needs to move to a niche instance. Not everyone needs to care about federation at the protocol level.

But if they do care later, the exit path exists.

That is the key distinction.

The goal is not to eliminate large instances. The goal is to make sure large instances are accountable, nonprofit, portable, and part of a wider network they do not own.

## Centralization Is Not Always the Enemy. Capture Is.

Fediverse discussions sometimes treat centralization as a binary: either everything is small and local, or we have recreated Big Tech.

I do not think that is right.

Some centralization is useful. Large instances can provide better uptime, better moderation coverage, better documentation, better support, and easier onboarding. They can give journalists, artists, public agencies, local organizations, and casual users a stable place to land.

The danger is not scale by itself.

The danger is capture.

A large nonprofit instance that exists within a federated network is very different from a venture-backed platform with investors, equity, growth targets, and eventual pressure to monetize. A nonprofit can still fail. It can still be mismanaged. It can still make bad moderation decisions. But it is not structurally designed to extract value from its users or sell itself to the highest bidder.

Even more importantly, it cannot buy the whole network.

That is the Fediverse’s underrated strength. There is no single company to acquire. No single boardroom can flip a switch and transform the entire network overnight. If one major instance goes bad, users can leave. Other servers can defederate. Communities can reorganize. The process may be messy, but the failure is contained.

The Fediverse does not need to avoid all centralization. It needs to avoid ownership concentration.

## Community Funded Does Not Have to Mean Hobby Funded

A lot of people hear “community funded” and imagine a tired admin paying server bills out of pocket.

That cannot be the long-term model for social infrastructure at scale.

If we want the Fediverse to support millions or hundreds of millions of people, community funding has to mature. That means recurring memberships. Foundation grants. Public-interest technology funds. University partnerships. Municipal and cultural institution support. Sponsorships that are clearly separated from editorial or moderation influence. Transparent budgets. Paid labor.

In other words, community funded does not mean amateur.

It means accountable.

There is a difference between a platform funded by users and one funded by advertisers. There is a difference between sponsorship recognition and surveillance advertising. There is a difference between public-interest infrastructure and growth-at-all-costs social media.

People already pay for streaming services, newsletters, cloud storage, podcasts, creator subscriptions, and productivity tools. The idea that nobody would ever pay a few dollars a month for healthier social infrastructure seems increasingly outdated.

The harder problem is not whether people can pay.

The harder problem is creating institutions they trust enough to pay.

## Moderation Requires Labor, Not Magic

Any serious discussion of Fediverse scale has to talk about moderation.

Moderation is not a feature you bolt on later. It is not something that can be solved entirely by protocol design. It is labor-intensive, emotionally draining, legally complicated, and culturally specific.

Small instances can do moderation through local trust and shared norms. That is one of their strengths. But large general-purpose instances need paid teams. They need escalation processes, coverage across time zones, documentation, tooling, and accountability. They need to handle abuse without burning out volunteers.

Regional nonprofit instances could help here because they would have a defined public-service mission and a funding model that supports staffing.

They could also coordinate with each other. Shared blocklists, trust and safety working groups, interoperable moderation tools, and transparent reporting could make the network safer without requiring one central authority.

This is where federation has an advantage that is easy to overlook. Moderation does not need to be either purely local or globally centralized. It can be layered.

A large regional instance can maintain its own rules. Smaller communities can maintain stricter ones. Users can choose clients, filters, and blocklists that match their needs. The result will never be perfect, but neither is corporate moderation. The difference is that the Fediverse can make moderation power more visible, distributed, and contestable.

## The Email Analogy Is Still Useful

People often compare the Fediverse to email, and for good reason.

Most people do not run their own mail server. Most people use a large provider. Some use Gmail, Outlook, Proton, Fastmail, university email, workplace email, or a custom domain. The average user does not need to understand SMTP to send a message.

But the underlying system is still interoperable.

That is roughly the shape federated social media should aim for.

There can be large default providers. There can be small community servers. There can be institutional servers. There can be personal servers. There can be regional nonprofit providers. Users can participate at whatever level of complexity they want.

The important thing is that no single provider owns the social layer.

A person should be able to start on a large general-purpose instance, learn the culture, and later move to a smaller community if they want. Or they can stay where they are. Both should be fine.

Success does not mean every user becomes a federation nerd. Success means users benefit from federation even when they do not think about it.

## This Is a Better Path Than Waiting for Benevolent Platforms

There is a recurring pattern in social media: a new platform arrives, promises to be different, gains trust from people tired of the old platform, and then asks users to wait for the truly decentralized future.

Maybe portability is coming. Maybe independent providers are coming. Maybe open infrastructure is coming. Maybe governance will improve later. Maybe the business model will remain benign forever.

But users have seen this movie before.

A technical escape hatch is not enough if the actual ecosystem remains centralized. If most users, clients, moderation systems, discovery tools, and social graph visibility still depend on one company, then the network is only decentralized in the ways least visible to normal people.

The Fediverse has the opposite problem. It is messy and uneven, but its decentralization is real. It exists socially, operationally, and politically. You can see it in the thousands of instances, the different moderation cultures, the community forks, the independent developers, and the fact that no one can buy the whole thing.

That is worth building on.

The answer to the Fediverse’s flaws should not be to retreat back into corporate platforms with better branding. The answer should be to professionalize the parts of the Fediverse that are currently under-resourced.

## What This Could Look Like in Practice

Imagine a few large nonprofit instances serving as default onramps:

- A North American general-purpose nonprofit instance
- A European general-purpose nonprofit instance
- Regional language-based instances
- Public-interest instances for journalists, researchers, artists, educators, or civic organizations
- Partnerships with libraries, universities, public broadcasters, and digital rights groups

Each would publish:

- Annual budgets
- Moderation reports
- Governance structures
- Data policies
- Federation policies
- Staffing plans
- Sustainability goals

Users could join for free, with recurring memberships encouraged. Institutions could sponsor infrastructure without receiving control over timelines, feeds, or moderation. Grants could fund moderation tooling, accessibility work, search, migration tools, and onboarding improvements.

This would not replace small instances. It would make them more viable.

A healthier network needs both:

- Large stable entry points for casual users
- Smaller communities for people who want tighter norms and stronger local identity

The mistake is thinking we have to choose one.

## The Real Challenge Is Cultural

The technology matters. Protocols matter. Migration tools matter. Moderation tools matter. But the biggest shift may be cultural.

We need to stop treating social infrastructure as free.

If we want platforms that do not manipulate us with engagement algorithms, sell behavioral data, chase advertisers, or collapse when a billionaire gets bored, then we have to fund something else.

That does not mean everyone pays. Public goods rarely work that way. Some users will contribute money. Others will contribute labor, code, documentation, moderation, art, reporting, governance, or simply healthy participation.

But enough people and institutions have to pay to make the system durable.

The Fediverse does not need to become Big Tech to reach global scale. It needs public-interest institutions with enough money, staff, legitimacy, and humility to serve as reliable infrastructure.

Not one giant platform. Not ten thousand exhausted hobby servers. Something in between.

A plural network with professional anchors.

## Conclusion: Build Institutions, Not Empires

The Fediverse is often criticized for being fragmented, confusing, and too dependent on volunteer labor. Those criticisms are not entirely wrong. But they are not arguments against federation. They are arguments for making federation more sustainable.

Large regional nonprofit instances could provide the missing middle layer: simple enough for casual users, funded enough to be reliable, accountable enough to deserve trust, and federated enough to avoid capture.

That model will not solve every problem. It will not eliminate moderation conflict, technical complexity, funding stress, or governance disputes. But neither will venture capital. Neither will another centralized platform promising that decentralization is just around the corner.

The Fediverse does not need to win by becoming seamless, frictionless, and growth-obsessed.

It can win by becoming durable.

It can win by treating social media as public infrastructure instead of a market to dominate.

It can win by giving people somewhere stable to start, and somewhere else to go if they need to leave.

That is the promise worth building toward.
